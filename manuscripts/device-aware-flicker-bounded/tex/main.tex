\author{Felipe Santib\'a\~nez-Leal~\orcidlink{0000-0002-0150-3246}\\
\small CAOS open-research programme, Santiago, Chile\\
\small \texttt{fsantibanez@gmail.com}}
\date{}
\maketitle
\vspace{-1.2em}
\headerblock

\begin{abstract}
Large arrays of optical traps are computed with phase-fixed weighted Gerchberg--Saxton (WGS) on an ideal model of the spatial
light modulator (SLM), and sequences that move traps interpolate between holograms never optimized for the frames between them. We test two proposed methods against phase-fixed WGS and linear phase interpolation on one scalar Fourier model.
Device-aware WGS keeps the WGS weights and replaces the phase update by conjugate-gradient steps through the pixel-crosstalk kernel
and the phase quantizer of the device. On a $10\times10$ lattice behind a crosstalk device it lowers the trap-intensity spread from a median of \sigmaMsevenThirty{} percent to \sigmaBOne{} percent, a geometric-mean factor of \ratioOneGm{}
(95 percent interval \ratioOneCiLo{} to \ratioOneCiHi) over \nSeedsOne{} seeds at \effRatioOne{} of its efficiency. The gain does not carry to every
geometry: on the device of the studio's benchmark, \benchOneDevPass{} of \benchOneDevN{} geometries meet both thresholds. An ablation shows that
the device-aware phase step produces the gain; the variant without the weights, which is conjugate-gradient minimization through the crosstalk model after Schroff et al.\ applied to point traps, has the lower spread here and a mixed one on the benchmark lattices. Flicker-bounded sequences
optimize every frame with the trap phases locked to a smooth trajectory and keep the lowest moving trap at \bTwoMean{} of its mean
intensity (95 percent interval \bTwoCiLo{} to \bTwoCiHi) against \lpiMean{} for linear phase interpolation over \nSeedsTwo{} seeds. With frames of peak step at most \claimStepFu{} Fourier units the bound is met on \longAMet{} and \longBMet{} of \longSeeds{} seeds of two long lattice moves; at equal frames on a long rearrangement, linear phase interpolation leads. Both
results are simulated, not measured on hardware.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} holographic optical tweezers, weighted Gerchberg--Saxton, pixel crosstalk, phase quantization, linear phase interpolation, atom rearrangement.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A phase-only SLM placed in the pupil plane of a lens forms an array of optical traps whose number now reaches the thousands. The largest
published array holds about 6100 atoms in about 12,000 tweezers, and the same report states that the light field is one of the limits of
the machine: the trap depth varies by 11.4 percent (standard deviation) across the sites after weighted GS optimization, the finite number of SLM pixels
bounds the array, and aberrations grow with the tweezer power~\cite{manetsch}. A small calibrated array reaches a relative standard deviation
of the intensity of 0.3 percent~\cite{chew}, so the spread grows with the size of the array and with the distance between the model that computes the hologram
and the device that displays it. Error-corrected neutral-atom processors of hundreds of physical qubits~\cite{bluvstein} and hybrid architectures that keep more than 3000 atoms for hours with a
static SLM array~\cite{chiu} make the quality and the speed of the hologram a question of the experiment's yield, not of its elegance.

The hologram is computed, not designed, and three families of method dominate large arrays. The Gerchberg--Saxton iteration and its weighted form~\cite{dileonardo}
equalize the trap intensities in the ideal model; freezing the focal-plane phase after a few iterations (phase-fixed WGS)~\cite{kim2019} keeps the weights effective and reaches
percent-level uniformity on arrays of 1500 foci; the same algorithm computes the holograms of the arrays of about 1000 tweezers and of 11,998 sites~\cite{ebadi,manetsch}. Camera feedback then
corrects the residual spread on the experiment~\cite{nogrette,kim2019}. The sequences that rearrange atoms with an SLM interpolate the position, the amplitude and the optical phase of
every trap between two endpoint holograms and invert the transform at every step~\cite{lpi}.

Two gaps follow. First, the solver of the large-array methods has no model of the device. The pixel crosstalk, the phase quantization and the finite fill factor of a real SLM change the trap
intensities after the hologram is computed, and the experiments absorb the change with camera iterations. A model of crosstalk was placed in conjugate-gradient minimization of shaped potentials~\cite{schroff}
and a convolution of the phase was placed in a weighted GS that, per its abstract, replaces amplitudes only~\cite{persson}, but no source puts a crosstalk model and a quantizer inside the weighted GS for point traps and
compares it with phase-fixed WGS. Second, the intermediate holograms of a moving-trap sequence are never individually optimized; their trap intensities are uneven and the interference of the transient
spots makes the intensity of a moving trap dip, down to a level that the source of the baseline links to atom loss~\cite{lpi}.

We propose and test one method for each gap and report them as they are: with their intervals, with the baselines run by the same code on the same data, with an ablation of their
components, and with the conditions under which they fail. The contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Device-aware weighted GS: the WGS weights, measured on the device's focal field, combined with conjugate-gradient steps on a device-aware loss whose gradient is closed form through the adjoint
of the crosstalk operator, with a safeguard that returns the best admissible iterate (Section~\ref{sec:b1}).
\item Flicker-bounded phase-continuous sequences: every intermediate hologram optimized with the trap phases locked to a smooth trajectory, together with a closed form for the transient intensity of a trap in the
blend model and the frame count that the closed form implies (Section~\ref{sec:b2}, Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item A controlled comparison: both methods run against phase-fixed WGS and linear phase interpolation implemented in the same engine on the same tasks, with seeds, intervals, the benchmark geometries of the studio
and long moves (Section~\ref{sec:design}, Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item An ablation measured by a committed script, whose main outcome reverses the expected role of a component: the device-aware phase step carries the gain of device-aware WGS, and the weights lower it on the acceptance task, while on the lattices of the benchmark's device neither form dominates (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item The scope of each claim, stated with the geometries and moves where it fails (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:related} places the methods among the published ones, Section~\ref{sec:problem} fixes the model, the device and the metrics, Section~\ref{sec:methods} defines the two methods,
Section~\ref{sec:theory} gives the propositions that explain them, and Sections~\ref{sec:design} to~\ref{sec:ablation} give the experiments. Section~\ref{sec:repro} lists the commands that rebuild every number.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Analytic and iterative solvers for point traps}

The point-trap model of Di Leonardo et al. computes the field at each trap as a sum over SLM pixels and compares the solvers on a ten by ten lattice on a 768 by 768 pixel SLM. In simulation,
plain GS reaches an efficiency of 0.94 and a uniformity of 0.60, and the weighted form reaches 0.93 and 0.99 in about 30 steps, with efficiency above 90 percent already after 10 steps~\cite{dileonardo}. Bianchi and
Di Leonardo moved the random-superposition start and the weighted iteration to a GPU~\cite{bianchi}. Direct search changes one pixel at a time: Polin et al. reached less than 5 percent deviation on 119 quasi-periodic
traps where GS and the adaptive additive variant stayed above 50 percent~\cite{polin}, at a cost that Di Leonardo et al. measured as about three times that of GS for an eight-level phase~\cite{dileonardo}.
For large arrays, Kim et al. observed that the weights lose their effect once the SLM-plane amplitude is replaced by the laser amplitude, and froze the focal-plane phases after an initial number of iterations; on 1500 foci they
report a modulation efficiency of 91.2 percent and a non-uniformity below 0.5 percent in simulation~\cite{kim2019}. The same rule computed the holograms of the arrays of about 1000 tweezers~\cite{ebadi} and the weights of the
array of 11,998 sites~\cite{manetsch}. The open library slmsuite implements the same weighted iteration with and without camera feedback and serves as an independent check of an implementation~\cite{slmsuite}. All of
these solvers optimize on an ideal model: the field they equalize is not the field the device produces.

\subsection{Camera feedback}

Feedback closes the gap between the model and the device by rescaling the target with the measured trap intensities. Nogrette et al. reduced the root-mean-square depth non-uniformity of ten by ten traps from 19 percent to 1.4 percent in 20
iterations with a gain of about 0.7~\cite{nogrette}. Kim et al. reduced a 22 percent non-uniformity to 1.59 percent in three steps with the phase-fixed rule, against 6.47 percent for conventional WGS~\cite{kim2019}. Chew et al. report a
relative standard deviation of 0.3 percent on a small array~\cite{chew}, and Wang et al. a non-uniformity below 1.1 percent for arrays above 1000 traps~\cite{wang}; both numbers are taken from abstracts. Feedback needs the experiment
and several camera exposures, and it starts from the hologram that the solver returns. A solver whose output is already flat on the device would shorten the loop; this is an expectation that the present paper does not test, since no
camera is in the loop here. The two loops start from different spreads, 19 and 22 percent, and both end at about one percent, so a start that is flat on the device model would begin the loop closer to its end than a start that is flat only on the ideal model.

\subsection{Cost-function, shaped-field, optimal-transport and learned holography}

Methods that minimize a cost over the full field serve shaped targets. The mixed-region amplitude freedom method of Pasienski and DeMarco lowers the error on six shaped targets to 0.015 to 0.039 against 0.21 to 0.36 for GS, at an efficiency of 0.18 to 0.45
against 0.96 to 0.99~\cite{pasienski}. Conjugate-gradient minimization of a power of the intensity error removes the vortices that the basic cost leaves, reaching 0.07 percent after 13,000 iterations on a ring~\cite{harte}, and a later variant controls phase and
amplitude together~\cite{bowman}. Optimal-transport initialization lowers the error of GS and of the mixed-region method on the same six targets, for example to 0.17 to 0.96 percent with an efficiency of 53 to 85 percent for the latter~\cite{swan}, and a dual
formulation reduces the memory to a linear order so that a 1024 by 1024 problem runs in 76 s on a CPU~\cite{torchylo}. Gradient methods with Wirtinger derivatives optimize any differentiable propagation model~\cite{chakravarthula}, and neural holography adds a calibrated
model and a camera in the loop~\cite{peng}. Learned generators are faster: DeepCGH is at least ten times faster than GS and 9 to 43 percent more accurate on multi-plane volumes~\cite{eybposh}, a network below 620 KB runs at video rate~\cite{shi}, and a network for about 20 tweezers
needs 0.16 s per hologram against about 6 s for GS, with about 10 percent variation across sites~\cite{ren}. None of these reports the uniformity of hundreds of point traps against phase-fixed WGS on the same array.

\subsection{Device models inside the solver}

The device appears in the optimization in three ways. Schroff et al. model the pixel crosstalk by upscaling each SLM pixel to three by three computational pixels and convolving the phase with a kernel fitted to a device, and minimize a normalized
intensity error with conjugate gradients and automatic differentiation; the model lowered the error of a large disc by a factor of about 0.4, lowered the efficiency by about 20 percent, made the run about six times longer (about 10 minutes per hologram), and was
not compared with a weighted GS~\cite{schroff}. Persson et al. modify a weighted GS for crosstalk, modelled as a low-pass filter of the phase retardation, and report more than 100 percent improvement of the spot uniformity on a device with large crosstalk; as far as the
abstract shows, the correction replaces amplitudes only~\cite{persson}. The flicker of a liquid-crystal device is a measured property: a twisted-nematic device shows phase fluctuations of about 120 degrees at one gray level~\cite{lizana}. Aberrations are the other device
effect: a position-dependent correction extends the aberration-free field of view~\cite{machu,christen}. No source found combines a crosstalk model and a quantizer with the weights of a weighted GS on point traps.

\subsection{Moving traps and rearrangement}

Atoms are sorted by moving traps. The hybrid architectures use acousto-optic deflectors for the fast moves of a few atoms on top of a static SLM array~\cite{endres,barredo,chiu}, and an SLM-only approach was proposed with GS, zero padding and weighted feedback~\cite{kim2019oe}.
The linear phase interpolation of the source of our baseline computes two endpoint WGS holograms, interpolates the position, amplitude and optical phase of each trap and inverts the transform at each step; with 13 holograms in about 40 ms it sorts a six by six array into
four by four, observes that the spot of independent WGS holograms nearly vanished several times at 11 kHz sampling while the interpolated spot stayed above 70 percent, and links phase slips near $\pi$ to atom loss~\cite{lpi}. The assignment of atoms to sites is a Hungarian matching whose exponent
on the distance avoids trespassing~\cite{lee2017}. No source optimizes the intermediate frames of a trap trajectory. The 3D extension of GS propagates plane by plane~\cite{whyte}; the present paper stays in two dimensions.

\subsection{Position of the proposed methods}

Table~\ref{tab:related} lists the works that matter for the two gaps with what each assumes and where it falls short. Device-aware WGS takes the weights of Di Leonardo et al.\ and the device model of Schroff et al.\ and joins them in a point-trap solver, which neither source does, and it is
compared with phase-fixed WGS, the method that computes the holograms of the largest arrays. Flicker-bounded sequences take the phase-fixed rule of Kim et al.\ and the interpolation insight of the linear-phase source, and optimize each frame, which the source does not. Both are
combinations of published pieces and are not claimed as new algorithms in isolation. The nearest published approach to device-aware WGS is conjugate-gradient minimization through a crosstalk model~\cite{schroff}, and the variant of device-aware WGS without the weight update is that approach transposed to point traps on bins (our implementation of the idea, not the authors' code or loss); the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that this variant has the lower spread on the acceptance task, so the weights are not what the paper contributes there. What the paper contributes is the transposition and its measurement against phase-fixed WGS, the closed forms that explain the floor and the safeguard, the measurement of where it holds and fails, and, for sequences, the per-frame optimization with locked phases.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Forward model of the traps}

The SLM has $n_{\mathrm{pix}}\times n_{\mathrm{pix}}$ pixels of pitch $p$ and a Gaussian input beam of amplitude $A_j$ normalized so that $\sum_jA_j^2=n_{\mathrm{pix}}^2$. A hologram is a phase $\phi_j\in[0,2\pi)$ per pixel $j$ at
position $(x_j,y_j)$. For a trap $m$ at $(x_m,y_m)$ in the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$ at wavelength $\lambda$, with $z_m=0$ and no vortex, the field and the intensity are
\begin{equation}
V_m=\frac1{n_{\mathrm{pix}}^2}\sum_jA_je^{\,i(\phi_j-\Delta_j^m)},\qquad \Delta_j^m=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda f}(x_jx_m+y_jy_m),\qquad I_m=|V_m|^2,\label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
following Di Leonardo et al.~\cite{dileonardo}. For traps on the bins of the zero-padded fast Fourier transform (FFT) of the SLM field, $V_m$ is the corresponding entry of that transform. The traps are on bins throughout: the lattices
are spaced a whole number of bins apart, and the focal bin is $\lambda f/(\mathrm{pad}\,n_{\mathrm{pix}}\,p)$ with a padding factor $\mathrm{pad}$. The unit of length in the focal plane for moves is the Fourier unit $\lambda f/(n_{\mathrm{pix}}p)$, equal to
$\mathrm{pad}$ bins. Each trap has a design weight $\omega_m$ (equal for all traps in every task of the paper), a design amplitude $a_m=\sqrt{\omega_m/\langle\omega\rangle}$ and a relative amplitude $r_m=|V_m|/a_m$. Equation~\ref{eq:forward} is the model that every hologram of the paper is computed on in the ideal case and scored on when the device is the ideal one; the device of the next subsection changes what is displayed and leaves the equation as the definition of what a displayed phase produces at a trap. The equation is linear in the unit phasors $e^{i\phi_j}$, which is what makes the gradient of the intensity loss available in closed form later.

\subsection{The device}

A real SLM displays something other than the hologram that is computed. The displayed phase is wrapped, quantized to $2^b$ levels and blurred by the crosstalk between neighbouring pixels, and the pixel covers only part of its area. We use the device model
of the studio~\cite{schroff}: each SLM pixel is $u\times u$ computational pixels, and the phase is convolved with the kernel
\begin{equation}
K=\mathcal F^{-1}\Big\{\exp\Big[-\frac{|k_x|^q+|k_y|^q}{\sigma_K^q}\Big]\Big\},\qquad \sigma_K=\parXtSigma,\ q=\parXtQ,\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
the values fitted by Schroff et al.\ on one device (Fig.~\ref{fig:b1model}a). With $U$ the replication of each pixel into $u\times u$ blocks and $\tilde\phi=K*(U\phi)$ the blurred phase on the fine grid, the focal field of what the device displays is
\begin{equation}
E=\mathrm{crop}\,\mathcal F\{\tilde A\,e^{i\tilde\phi}\}\cdot\mathcal E_{\mathrm{fill}},\qquad V_m=E[\text{bin of }m]/\mathrm{scale},\label{eq:devicefield}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde A$ is the amplitude on the fine grid, the crop returns the field to the window of the ideal FFT and $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{fill}}$ is the field envelope of a square pixel of fill factor $\mathrm{fill}$. Two devices are used. The
\emph{crosstalk device} has the kernel above, no quantization and a fill factor of one. The \emph{full device} adds a \parBits{}-bit quantizer and a fill factor of \parFill. The device of the studio's benchmark and of case C08 adds a random flicker of \bakeCeightFlicker{} rad rms
to the displayed phase. Every hologram in the paper is scored on the device with the same function, whatever model it was optimized on, so a method is never scored on its own model unless the device and the model coincide.

\subsection{Figures of merit}

The efficiency is the light in the traps, and the spread is the relative standard deviation of the intensities divided by the design weights, in percent:
\begin{equation}
e=\sum_mI_m,\qquad \sigma=100\,\frac{\operatorname{std}_m(I_m/\omega_m)}{\operatorname{mean}_m(I_m/\omega_m)}.\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
The comparison of a method with the reference solver on the same seed uses two ratios, the factor on the spread and the ratio of the efficiencies,
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{\min\{\sigma_{30},\sigma_{100}\}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{DA}}},\qquad R_e=\frac{e_{\mathrm{DA}}}{\max\{e_{30},e_{100}\}},\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
where the subscripts 30 and 100 name the iteration budgets of phase-fixed WGS, so the reference is the best of the two runs on the spread and on the efficiency, and the comparison is never taken against a baseline that more iterations would improve. The spread of Eq.~\ref{eq:metrics} is the quantity that the experiments of the sources report as non-uniformity or as relative standard deviation, which makes the numbers of the paper comparable in kind with theirs; the uniformity defined through the maximum and the minimum intensity is not used, because it depends on two traps only. Dividing the intensity by the design weight lets the same formula serve arrays whose traps are meant to differ, although every task here has equal traps. The efficiency ratio of Eq.~\ref{eq:factor} protects the factor from a trivial gain: a hologram that sent light to the zero order would flatten the traps at a low efficiency, and the ratio would show it.

\subsection{Moving traps and the transient intensity}

A sequence has $S+1$ frames $s=0,\dots,S$ and a trap position $x_m(s)$ that follows a time profile $h(\tau)$ with $\tau=s/S$. The SLM does not jump from one hologram to the next, and a trap that moves is read at its position between the frames. We model the
displayed field between frames $s$ and $s+1$ as the blend of the two holograms, $f_\alpha=A[(1-\alpha)e^{i\phi_s}+\alpha e^{i\phi_{s+1}}]$ with $\alpha\in[0,1]$ ($\parSubsteps$ sub-frames), and read trap $m$ at its trajectory point $x_m(s+\alpha)$. With
$I_m(s,\alpha)=|V_m(f_\alpha;x_m(s+\alpha))|^2$ the transient ratio of a trap is
\begin{equation}
\rho_m=\frac{\min_{s,\alpha}I_m(s,\alpha)}{\operatorname{mean}_sI_m(s)}.\label{eq:transient}
\end{equation}
The blend model is our own definition: the source of the baseline gives a measurement, not an equation, so the absolute value of $\rho_m$ is a property of the model and the methods are compared with each other only. Both sequences are scored by the same function. The numerator of Eq.~\ref{eq:transient} is the lowest intensity that trap $m$ takes over the frames and over the blend instants, and the denominator is its mean intensity at the frames, so $\rho_m=1$ means that the trap never dips below its mean and a value of $0.9$ means that the lowest transient is a tenth below it. The blend represents the liquid crystal relaxing between two holograms; the refresh rate and the temporal response of the device are not modelled, so $\rho_m$ is a property of the pair of holograms and of the trajectory and not of an instrument.

\subsection{Notation and assumptions}

Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the symbols. The assumptions are the following. The model is scalar and monochromatic; the traps lie on FFT bins in one plane; the crosstalk parameters are those fitted on another device by the source, and their frequency unit is
carried over from the model and is not verified against the source; the quantizer is differentiated as the identity (straight-through) in the optimization, and the random flicker is scored but not modelled by the optimizer; the transient model is the blend above. Each of these limits is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limits}. A further assumption concerns the baselines: phase-fixed WGS is run on the ideal model and scored on the device, which is the practice of the large-array experiments, where the solver is not given a device model and the device is corrected afterwards by camera feedback. The comparison is therefore the one that matters before feedback, and the weights, the starts and the random numbers of every method come from the same seeded generator.

\section{Proposed methods}\label{sec:methods}

The two methods share the point-trap model of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, the weighted update of Di Leonardo et al.~\cite{dileonardo} and the phase-fixed rule of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2019}, and they differ in the gap they close. Device-aware weighted GS closes the gap between the model
that computes a static hologram and the device that displays it. Flicker-bounded sequences close the gap between consecutive holograms of a moving array. Both run offline: they are baked into the studio's artifacts, and the live engine of the studio does not
carry them, because the cost of one iteration exceeds the budget of an interactive frame. Each subsection gives the idea, the derivation, the algorithm, the parameters with their values and the way they were chosen, the complexity and the implementation.

\subsection{Device-aware weighted GS}\label{sec:b1}

\subsubsection{Idea}

Weighted GS equalizes the trap intensities because its weights integrate the relative error of each trap: a trap that is too bright has its weight lowered at the next iteration, and the fixed point of the update is the set of equal amplitudes. On an ideal SLM the equalization is exact
to the precision of the arithmetic. On a device the intensities that the weights see are those of the ideal model, so the weights equalize a field that the device does not produce. Two changes follow from this observation. First, the amplitudes $r_m$ that drive the weights are read
on the device's focal field (Eq.~\ref{eq:devicefield}), so the integral action acts on the quantity that is scored. Second, the projection that produces the new phase from the weighted trap fields is an ideal-model operation, and no choice of weights can make the projection invert the crosstalk;
we replace it by a few steps of gradient descent on a loss that is evaluated on the device and differentiated through it. The weights remain, because they supply a target that moves with the error, and the safeguard keeps the method from ever returning a hologram worse than its start.

\subsubsection{Derivation}

Let $V_m(\phi)$ be the trap fields of the device (Eq.~\ref{eq:devicefield}) as a function of the SLM phase. Writing the blurred phase on the fine grid as $\tilde\phi=K*(U\phi)$, the field of trap $m$ is linear in the unit phasors $e^{i\tilde\phi_p}$ of the fine pixels $p$,
\begin{equation}
V_m=\sum_pG_{mp}\,e^{i\tilde\phi_p},\qquad G_{mp}=c_m\tilde A_p\,e^{-i\tilde\Delta_p^m},\label{eq:linear}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde\Delta_p^m$ is the delay of Eq.~\ref{eq:forward} on the fine grid and $c_m$ collects the readout scale and the pixel envelope of trap $m$. The loss compares the trap intensities with a target $T_m$ that depends on the weights,
\begin{equation}
L(\phi)=\frac{\sum_m\big(|V_m(\phi)|^2-T_m\big)^2}{\sum_mT_m^2},\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
T_m=\eta\,\frac{(w_ma_m)^2}{\sum_k(w_ka_k)^2},\qquad \eta=\sum_m|V_m|^2.\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
The level $\eta$ is the current efficiency, so the target has the same total light as the hologram: the steps redistribute the light among the traps and do not trade efficiency for uniformity. The weights follow the rule of Di Leonardo et al., with the relative amplitudes read on the device,
\begin{equation}
w_m\leftarrow w_m\frac{\langle r\rangle}{r_m},\qquad r_m=\frac{|V_m|}{a_m}.\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
The gradient of $L$ is obtained in two steps. Let $N_T=\sum_mT_m^2$, $d_m=|V_m|^2-T_m$ and $h_p=\sum_mG_{mp}d_m\overline{V_m}$. Differentiating $L$ with respect to the blurred phase of the fine pixel $p$ gives
\begin{equation}
\tilde g_p=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\tilde\phi_p}=\frac{4}{N_T}\operatorname{Re}\!\big(i\,e^{i\tilde\phi_p}\,h_p\big),\label{eq:gradfine}
\end{equation}
and the chain rule through the replication and the blur gives the gradient with respect to the SLM phase,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\phi L=U^{\top}\!\big(\bar K*\tilde g\big),\qquad (U^{\top}z)_j=\sum_{p\in\text{block }j}z_p,\label{eq:gradphi}
\end{equation}
with $\bar K$ the reversed kernel. The matrix $G$ never needs to be formed: $h$ is one inverse transform of the readout of the weighted residual, and Eq.~\ref{eq:gradphi} is a convolution followed by a sum over each $u\times u$ block. This is the adjoint of the crosstalk operator, which the
engine tests against the inner-product identity to $10^{-12}$, so no automatic differentiation is needed and the gradient is exact for the smooth part of the model. The quantizer is treated as the identity in the backward pass and is applied in the forward pass (straight-through), and the random flicker is not
modelled. The minimization of $L$ over $\phi$ with $T$ fixed uses the Fletcher--Reeves conjugate-gradient recursion with a strong-Wolfe line search,
\begin{equation}
d_i=-g_i+\beta_id_{i-1},\quad \beta_i=\frac{g_i^{\top}g_i}{g_{i-1}^{\top}g_{i-1}},\quad \phi_{i+1}=\phi_i+\alpha_id_i,\label{eq:cg}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_i$ satisfying $L(\phi_i+\alpha_id_i)\le L(\phi_i)+c_1\alpha_ig_i^{\top}d_i$ and $|g(\phi_i+\alpha_id_i)^{\top}d_i|\le c_2|g_i^{\top}d_i|$ for $c_1=10^{-4}$ and $c_2=0.1$; the choice $c_2<1/2$ keeps the Fletcher--Reeves direction a descent direction.
The recursion restarts at every outer iteration, because the target of Eq.~\ref{eq:target} changes with the weights and the conjugacy of the old directions no longer holds.

The weight update acts as an integrator and a few gradient steps may not follow it on a device that quantizes and blurs the response. The method therefore keeps every iterate $\phi^k$, $k=0,\dots,K_{\mathrm{out}}$, with $\phi^0$ the start, and returns the iterate of lowest spread on its own device model among those that keep a fraction $\gamma$ of the start's efficiency,
\begin{equation}
k^\star=\arg\min_{k\in\mathcal A}\sigma\big(\phi^k\big),\qquad \mathcal A=\{k:\ e(\phi^k)\ge\gamma\,e(\phi^0)\}\ni0,\label{eq:safeguard}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=\parFloor$, the bound of the efficiency in the hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:design}. Because the start belongs to $\mathcal A$, the returned hologram is never worse than the start on the model of the method.

Three remarks read the equations. First, Eq.~\ref{eq:loss} is a loss on intensities, not on amplitudes: the intensities are what the scoring of Eq.~\ref{eq:metrics} compares, and the loss does not change under a global phase of the field, so the optimization has no gauge freedom to fix. Second, the normalization by $\sum_mT_m^2$ makes the loss independent of the scale of the target, so the line search can start from a step that moves the largest pixel by a fixed fraction of a radian whatever the number of traps. Third, Eq.~\ref{eq:target} fixes the total of the target to the current efficiency $\eta$: when a step lowers the light in the traps the next target is lower, so the loss never rewards efficiency that is lost to the zero order or to ghost orders, and the efficiency is guarded only by the floor of Eq.~\ref{eq:safeguard}. In the ideal model, with $K$ the identity, $u=1$ and no quantizer, Eq.~\ref{eq:gradphi} reduces to the gradient of the intensity-error cost that conjugate-gradient hologram generation minimizes~\cite{harte}, so device-aware WGS contains that cost as its special case without a device.

The construction has limits that follow from the equations. The quantizer is a staircase: its derivative vanishes almost everywhere, so the straight-through gradient is a good direction for the smooth part of the model and carries no information about the steps of the staircase, and the full device gains less than the crosstalk device (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The readout takes the nearest FFT bin, so a trap off a bin would be read with an error, and the method refuses traps off the focal plane and vortex traps. The loss is not convex, the conjugate-gradient steps find a local descent and the result depends on the start; the start is therefore fixed to the weighted iterations from the random superposition of the seed, and the seeds of the experiments sample it.

\subsubsection{Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:b1} states the procedure. The start is $K_{\mathrm{warm}}$ iterations of weighted GS on the ideal model from the random superposition of prisms and lenses, which is the number of weighted iterations that Kim et al.\ run before they freeze the phases~\cite{kim2019}.
Each outer iteration reads the trap fields on the device, updates the weights, sets the target and takes $K_{\mathrm{cg}}$ steps.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Device-aware weighted GS. The device model $\mathcal D$ is the composition of replication, blur, FFT on the fine grid, crop and pixel envelope; $\mathcal D=\mathrm{id}$ gives the ideal model.}
\label{alg:b1}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require traps $\{x_m,\omega_m\}$ on FFT bins, device $\mathcal D$, outer iterations $K_{\mathrm{out}}$, steps $K_{\mathrm{cg}}$, warm iterations $K_{\mathrm{warm}}$, floor $\gamma$, seed
\Ensure phase $\phi^\star\in[0,2\pi)$ per SLM pixel
\State $\phi^0\gets K_{\mathrm{warm}}$ iterations of weighted GS on the ideal model from the random superposition (seeded)
\State $w_m\gets1$; compute $V(\phi^0)$ on $\mathcal D$; $e_0\gets e(\phi^0)$; $\sigma_0\gets\sigma(\phi^0)$; best $\gets(\phi^0,\sigma_0)$
\For{$k=1,\dots,K_{\mathrm{out}}$}
  \State $V\gets V(\phi^{k-1})$ on $\mathcal D$; $r_m\gets|V_m|/a_m$; $w_m\gets w_m\langle r\rangle/r_m$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:weights}}
  \State $T_m\gets\eta(w_ma_m)^2/\sum_l(w_la_l)^2$ with $\eta=\sum|V_m|^2$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:target}}
  \State $\phi^k\gets K_{\mathrm{cg}}$ Fletcher--Reeves steps on $L$ from $\phi^{k-1}$ with strong-Wolfe search \Comment{Eqs.~\ref{eq:loss},~\ref{eq:gradphi},~\ref{eq:cg}}
  \State compute $e(\phi^k)$, $\sigma(\phi^k)$ on $\mathcal D$

\EndFor
\State \Return the phase of best, wrapped to $[0,2\pi)$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:safeguard}}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Complexity}

One conjugate-gradient step evaluates the forward model on the fine grid and its adjoint once per line-search trial, about $u^2$ times the pixels of the plain FFT, and the weights add a pass over the traps. With $N_{\mathrm{fine}}=u^2n_{\mathrm{pad}}^2$ the number of fine samples, a step costs
$O(N_{\mathrm{fine}}\log N_{\mathrm{fine}})$ in the FFT form, and the readout at the traps is $O(M)$. An outer iteration costs $K_{\mathrm{cg}}$ steps plus the line-search trials, and a run costs $K_{\mathrm{warm}}$ ideal iterations plus $K_{\mathrm{out}}$ outer iterations. The pruned transform
that the engine uses for the device-aware cost makes the transform about 2.4 times cheaper than the full one, and the source of the crosstalk model reports that the model alone makes a hologram about six times slower. A requirement of the research plan was that the time per iteration
stay within a factor of three of the baseline; this ratio was not measured, so it is not claimed. The acceptance test of the engine takes about half a minute on the development machine, which is why the method is baked and not live. The line search dominates the cost of a step: each trial evaluates the forward model once, the strong-Wolfe search may use several trials per step, and the engine caps the evaluations of one search at 25, so the cost of an outer iteration is $K_{\mathrm{cg}}$ steps times a number of evaluations between one and the cap. The evaluations are counted and returned in the metadata of a run, so the cost of a hologram can be read afterwards and not only estimated.

\subsubsection{Implementation}

The method is the module \texttt{b1\_device\_wgs.py} of the engine in the repository of the studio, release \engineRelease. The device model is the module \texttt{device.py}, whose adjoint passes the inner-product identity to $10^{-12}$ for three devices, and the conjugate-gradient routine is shared with the other
optimization methods of the studio. The traps must lie on FFT bins with $z=0$ and no vortex when a device is given, and the method refuses a shaped-field target. The ablation script of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} re-implements the loop with switchable components and checks that, with all components on, it returns the
phase of the module to a difference of \ablBCheck{} on the spread for every seed, so the ablated variants are the module with parts removed and not a second method. The result of a run carries the phase, a history with one entry per outer iteration (the loss, the efficiency and the spread), the trap fields on the device and the metadata of the run: the number of steps, the warm iterations, the evaluations, the final weights, the iteration that was returned, the floor and the spread and efficiency of the start. The history keeps every iterate so that a run that winds up can be inspected, and the convergence curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:b1model}b is read from it. The routine accepts an initial phase that replaces the warm start and raises an error for fewer than one step or a negative number of warm iterations. The random superposition of the start is drawn from the portable generator of the engine, so the result is a function of the seed.

\subsection{Flicker-bounded phase-continuous sequences}\label{sec:b2}

\subsubsection{Idea}

Linear phase interpolation builds the intermediate holograms of a move from two endpoint holograms and never optimizes them: a frame is the inverse transform of interpolated spots, so its trap intensities are uneven and the transient spots interfere. A weighted GS that is run independently at every
frame equalizes each frame but leaves the optical phase of every trap free, so the phases may jump between frames and the blend of two frames dips, which is the flicker that the source of the baseline observed. The proposed sequence combines the two remedies: it optimizes every frame, which equalizes the trap intensities, and it locks the
optical phase of every trap to a smooth trajectory, which removes the phase jumps. With both, the target field changes slowly from one frame to the next, so the holograms change slowly and so does the blend. The bound on the phase step per frame refuses a trajectory whose phases would have to slip too fast.

The relation to the two parents is as follows. Linear phase interpolation has smooth phases and unoptimized frames; weighted GS at every frame has optimized frames and free phases. In the engine's earlier measurement on a nine-trap sort, weighted GS at every step equalized each frame and showed no flicker (a median transient ratio of 0.96 in that model), unlike the 11 kHz camera observation of the source; that measurement is recorded in the design record and is not repeated here, and we do not use weighted GS at every frame as a baseline for that reason, comparing instead with the baseline that the source of the problem uses. The sequence we propose is not a claim that free phases always flicker; it is a construction whose phase path is controlled by design, with a measurement of what that control is worth in an ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsubsection{Derivation}

Positions follow the minimal-jerk profile, the polynomial that has zero velocity and acceleration at both ends,
\begin{equation}
x_m(s)=x_m(0)+\big(x_m(S)-x_m(0)\big)\,h(\tau),\qquad h(\tau)=10\tau^3-15\tau^4+6\tau^5,\qquad \tau=\frac sS.\label{eq:minjerk}
\end{equation}
The optical phases of the traps follow the same profile between the phases $\theta_m(0)$ of the first frame and end phases $\theta_m^{\mathrm{end}}$ that are given (by default the phases are held, $\theta^{\mathrm{end}}=\theta(0)$),
\begin{equation}
\theta_m(s)=\theta_m(0)+\operatorname{wrap}\big(\theta_m^{\mathrm{end}}-\theta_m(0)\big)\,h(\tau).\label{eq:phaseprofile}
\end{equation}
Every frame $s\ge1$ is a phase-fixed weighted GS step with the trap phases locked to Eq.~\ref{eq:phaseprofile}: with the trap set $x_m(s)$ and the coefficients $c_m=w_ma_me^{i\theta_m(s)}$,
\begin{equation}
\phi_j^{(s)}=\arg\sum_mc_m\,e^{+i\Delta_j^m(x_m(s))},\qquad w_m\leftarrow w_m\frac{\langle r\rangle}{r_m},\label{eq:frame}
\end{equation}
started from the hologram and the weights of frame $s-1$ and iterated $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ times. Frame 0 is a cold phase-fixed WGS of $K_0$ iterations whose trap phases give $\theta_m(0)$. The hard lock is the limit $\lambda\to\infty$ of a penalty $\lambda\sum_m(\arg V_m-\theta_m)^2$ added to the amplitude error, which the engine does not
form. The sequence is refused when the phase step per frame, the largest change of $\theta_m(s)$ between consecutive frames, exceeds a bound $\dot\theta_{\max}$,
\begin{equation}
\max_{m,s}\big|\theta_m(s+1)-\theta_m(s)\big|\le\dot\theta_{\max},\label{eq:phasebound}
\end{equation}
and the bound has a direct meaning for the target of the optimization: the coefficients of two consecutive frames differ by
\begin{equation}
\big|c_m(s+1)-c_m(s)\big|\le w_ma_m\,\big|\theta_m(s+1)-\theta_m(s)\big|+a_m\,\big|w_m(s+1)-w_m(s)\big|,\label{eq:targetstep}
\end{equation}
so a bounded phase step and weights that change little make the target field, and therefore the hologram that inverts it, change slowly from frame to frame. The number of frames that a move needs follows from the same profile. The velocity of $h$ is $h'(\tau)=30\tau^2(1-\tau)^2$, maximal at $\tau=\tfrac12$ where it equals $15/8$, so the largest shift between two consecutive frames of a trap that travels $L$ Fourier units in $S$ intervals is, to first order in $1/S$,
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\max}=\frac{15}{8}\,\frac{L}{S},\qquad S\ge\frac{15}{8}\,\frac{L}{\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}},\label{eq:frames}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}=\claimStepFu{}$ Fourier units the step bound of the claim. The engine returns the smallest number of frames that satisfies the second relation and is at least the default number of frames $F_0$,
\begin{equation}
F(L)=\max\Big\{F_0,\ \Big\lceil\frac{15}{8}\frac{L}{\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}}\Big\rceil+1\Big\}.\label{eq:framecount}
\end{equation}
The origin of $\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}$ is the closed form of Section~\ref{sec:theory} and a calibration on the rearrangement case, reported with
its limits in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

The equations read as follows. Equation~\ref{eq:minjerk} fixes where each trap is at each frame, and Eq.~\ref{eq:phaseprofile} fixes the optical phase that it must have there. Equation~\ref{eq:frame} is the usual weighted back-projection with one difference: the phase of each coefficient comes from the prescribed path and not from the current field, and this is the difference that gives the method its name. The weights and the hologram are carried from frame to frame, so every frame is a few iterations away from the previous one and not a new solution: with held phases and small steps, the problem of frame $s$ is a small perturbation of the problem of frame $s-1$. The weights integrate the amplitude error across frames as well as across iterations, which is the integral action of device-aware WGS again. In the penalty form the lock would be a soft constraint, and the optimizer could trade phase error for amplitude error; the hard lock refuses the trade. When end phases are given, Eq.~\ref{eq:phaseprofile} follows the wrapped difference, which is the short path on the circle and can differ from the path that the hologram at the destination would take on its own: the end phases are an input of the method and not a result of it. Between frames the displayed field is the blend of two holograms; the lock bounds the phase step, the optimization equalizes the frames, and the blend is then close to a hologram of an intermediate trap set.

\subsubsection{Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:b2} states the procedure. The transient ratios of Eq.~\ref{eq:transient} are computed after the last frame on the blend model, and they are the figure of merit of the sequence; they are not used inside the optimization.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Flicker-bounded sequence for a move from traps $X(0)$ to $X(S)$ with end phases $\theta^{\mathrm{end}}$ (held by default).}
\label{alg:b2}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require traps $X(0)$, destination $X(S)$ with the same ids, frames $S+1$, iterations $K_0$ and $K_{\mathrm{f}}$, bound $\dot\theta_{\max}$, seed
\Ensure holograms $\phi^{(0)},\dots,\phi^{(S)}$ and transient ratios $\rho_m$
\State $\phi^{(0)}\gets$ phase-fixed WGS of $K_0$ iterations on $X(0)$; $\theta_m(0)\gets\arg V_m(\phi^{(0)})$
\State $\theta_m(s)\gets$ Eq.~\ref{eq:phaseprofile}; \textbf{if} Eq.~\ref{eq:phasebound} fails \textbf{then} refuse the sequence
\State $w_m\gets1$; $\phi\gets\phi^{(0)}$
\For{$s=1,\dots,S$}
  \State place the traps at $x_m(s)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:minjerk}); $c_m\gets w_ma_me^{i\theta_m(s)}$
  \For{$t=1,\dots,K_{\mathrm{f}}$}
    \State $V_m\gets V_m(\phi)$ at $x_m(s)$; $w_m\gets w_m\langle r\rangle/r_m$; $\phi\gets\arg\sum_mw_ma_me^{i\theta_m(s)}e^{+i\Delta^m}$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:frame}}
  \EndFor
  \State $\phi^{(s)}\gets\phi\bmod2\pi$
\EndFor
\State compute $\rho_m$ from the blend of consecutive frames (Eq.~\ref{eq:transient}); \Return holograms and $\rho$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Parameters}

The number of frames, \parFrames, is the number of holograms that the source of the baseline uses to sort a six by six array into a four by four array. The number of iterations per frame, \parFrameIters, is an engine choice: the engine's acceptance test fails with no iteration, and the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation}
shows that the lowest transient ratio is already above the bound with three iterations and below it with one. The bound on the phase step per frame, \parPhaseVel{} rad, is an engine choice taken from the observation in the source that phase slips of several tenths of $\pi$ barely mattered for atom survival while slips near $\pi$ lost atoms; it is
a bound on the phase path and not a claim about atoms. The step bound of the claim, \claimStepFu{} Fourier units, is derived in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. The \parSubsteps{} sub-frames per interval of the transient model are a numerical resolution of the blend. Two parameters interact. With held phases the phase step is zero and the bound never binds, whereas with end phases the largest step is $\frac{15}{8}|\Delta\theta|/S$ for a phase difference $\Delta\theta$ (the same profile as Proposition~\ref{prop:frames}), which is why the experiments with end phases on long moves lift the bound from \parPhaseVel{} to \scopePhaseVel{} rad and why the default bound refuses a long move at the default number of frames. The state of the weights is carried across frames and not reset, because resetting it would discard the integral action. The freeze iteration of the phase-fixed rule at frame 0 is the smaller of the freeze iteration of Kim et al.\ and the cold-start budget.

\subsubsection{Complexity}

A sequence is $S$ warm-started runs of $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ iterations, each iteration being one forward projection and one back-projection of the point-trap form, $O(N_{\mathrm{pix}}M)$ for $M$ traps and $N_{\mathrm{pix}}$ SLM pixels, so the cost of the sequence is $O(SK_{\mathrm{f}}N_{\mathrm{pix}}M)$ after the cold start of frame 0. The frame count grows linearly with the length of the move (Eq.~\ref{eq:frames}),
and therefore so does the cost; this is the price of the bound and one of the limits of the method. The scoring of the transient ratio costs $\parSubsteps$ projections per interval. The source of the baseline reports a computation of under 200 microseconds per hologram on a GPU for its own method; we do not rely on that figure, and our runs are offline. The sequence also stores every hologram, so the memory of a run grows with the number of frames as well, and the transient scoring evaluates the blend at every sub-frame of every interval; for the longest move of the paper, with \cTenFrames{} frames, the scoring is a larger share of the run than for the acceptance move.

\subsubsection{Implementation}

The method is the module \texttt{b2\_flicker\_bounded.py} of the engine, which provides the profile, the trajectory, the transient scoring and the frame-count rule, and which takes the destination, the number of frames, the iterations per frame, the end phases and the bound as arguments. It works in the point-trap form, so the traps need not lie on bins, in two or three dimensions, on the
ideal device. The phase-fixed rule of the first frame and the weighted iteration are those of the ladder methods of the studio, so a correction of one reaches the other at once. The ablation script re-implements the sequence with switchable components and checks that with all components on it reproduces the lowest transient ratio of the module to a difference of \ablTCheck{} for every seed. The call returns the last hologram as the phase, a history with one entry per frame (efficiency, uniformity and spread), the trap fields of the last frame and, in the metadata, every frame, the transient ratio per trap, the mask of the moving traps (those with a displacement), the phases of the first frame, the iterations per frame and the number of frames. It raises an error for a shaped target, for a destination that does not hold the same trap identifiers in the same order, for end phases whose number differs from the number of traps, and for a phase step above the bound. The experiments read the lowest value over the moving traps, which is the quantity of the hypothesis, and score the traps that hold still through the per-frame spread.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

The propositions below explain why each method should work and under which conditions; where a statement could not be proved we state it as a conjecture with the evidence for it. They are used in the experiments: Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} predicts the floor that the baseline meets on the device, Proposition~\ref{prop:weights} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:guard} define what the weights and the safeguard guarantee, and Proposition~\ref{prop:blend} gives the closed form against which the measured transient ratios are read.

\subsection{Why an ideal-model solver leaves a floor on the device}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:floor}
Let $\phi$ be a hologram whose ideal-model trap fields are exactly equal, $|V_m^0|=a_m$, and let the device display the phase $U\phi+\delta$ on the fine grid, with $\delta=K*(U\phi)-U\phi$ the residual of the blur. To first order in $\delta$ the relative intensity error of trap $m$ on the device is
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_m=\frac{I_m-I_m^0}{I_m^0}=2\operatorname{Re}\frac{\Delta V_m}{V_m^0},\qquad \Delta V_m=i\sum_pG_{mp}\,e^{iU\phi_p}\,\delta_p,\label{eq:firstorder}
\end{equation}
and $\varepsilon_m=0$ for all traps if and only if $\operatorname{Re}(\Delta V_m/V_m^0)=0$ for all $m$, that is, if the blur leaves the trap intensities unchanged to first order, in particular if $K$ is the identity.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With the linear form of Eq.~\ref{eq:linear}, $V_m=\sum_pG_{mp}e^{i(U\phi_p+\delta_p)}=\sum_pG_{mp}e^{iU\phi_p}(1+i\delta_p+O(\delta^2))=V_m^0+\Delta V_m+O(\delta^2)$. Then $I_m=|V_m^0|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(\overline{V_m^0}\Delta V_m)+O(\delta^2)$, and dividing by $I_m^0$ gives Eq.~\ref{eq:firstorder}. The error vanishes for every trap exactly when
$\operatorname{Re}(\Delta V_m/V_m^0)=0$ for all $m$, which holds for $\delta=0$. The expansion treats $\delta$ as small, and a wrapped phase violates that at its jumps between $0$ and $2\pi$, where the blur is of the order of the jump; Eq.~\ref{eq:firstorder} is therefore a local statement about where the floor comes from, and the claims of the paper rest on the measured spreads and not on the first-order value.
\end{proof}

The proposition has two consequences. First, an ideal-model solver cannot remove $\varepsilon_m$: the weights of weighted GS are computed from $|V_m^0|$, which is already equal, so the iteration has converged on the ideal model and has no information about $\varepsilon_m$. More iterations improve the ideal-model spread and leave $\varepsilon_m$ unchanged, which is the control that
the baseline at 100 iterations provides. Second, the size of $\delta$ is set by the spectrum of the hologram: in the Fourier domain the blur multiplies the phase spectrum by the transfer function of Eq.~\ref{eq:kernel}, $\hat\delta(k)=(\hat K(k)-1)\widehat{U\phi}(k)$, which is small where the hologram is smooth and large where it carries
pixel-scale structure. A lattice of traps far from the zero order needs a hologram with such structure, so the floor is expected to depend on the geometry of the array; the measured spreads of the baseline on the lattice and on the other geometries are read against this expectation in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{What the weights, the steps and the safeguard guarantee}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weights}
The weight update of Eq.~\ref{eq:weights} is an integrator of the logarithmic relative amplitude: $\log w_m^{k+1}=\log w_m^k-(\log r_m^k-\log\langle r^k\rangle)$. A fixed point $w^\star$ of the update satisfies $r_m(w^\star)=\langle r\rangle$ for every trap, that is, equal relative amplitudes on the device, and conversely.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Taking logarithms of Eq.~\ref{eq:weights} gives the first statement. The update leaves $w_m$ unchanged if and only if $\langle r\rangle/r_m=1$, which is $r_m=\langle r\rangle$ for every $m$; equal relative amplitudes mean $\sigma=0$ for equal design weights.
\end{proof}

The proposition says what the weights can do and what they cannot: they drive the device to equal amplitudes if the iteration converges, and they act with the memory of an integrator, which is the reason why they are prone to overshoot when the plant that they control, a hologram that moves by a few gradient steps per iteration, does not follow. The next two statements make this precise.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:descent}
Within one outer iteration, with $T$ fixed, every accepted conjugate-gradient step lowers $L$, so $L(\phi^k)\le L(\phi^{k-1};T^k)$. Across outer iterations nothing bounds $L$: the target $T^{k+1}$ differs from $T^k$, and $L(\phi^k;T^{k+1})$ may exceed $L(\phi^k;T^k)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The strong-Wolfe line search accepts a step only under the sufficient-decrease condition of Eq.~\ref{eq:cg}, so $L$ decreases along the steps for a fixed $T$. The target is a function of the weights and of $\eta$, both of which are updated between outer iterations; the loss with the new target is a different function.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:guard}
The iterate returned by Eq.~\ref{eq:safeguard} satisfies $\sigma(\phi^{k^\star})\le\sigma(\phi^0)$ and $e(\phi^{k^\star})\ge\gamma\,e(\phi^0)$, both measured on the model of the method. If the model of the method is the scoring device, as for the crosstalk device and the full device, the same holds on the score.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The start belongs to $\mathcal A$ by definition, so the set is not empty and the minimum is over a set that contains the start. The minimizer has a spread no larger than the start's and, being in $\mathcal A$, an efficiency of at least $\gamma e(\phi^0)$. When the scoring device differs from the model, for example by random flicker that is scored and not modelled, the
statement holds on the model and not on the score.
\end{proof}

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:windup}
When the conjugate-gradient steps per outer iteration cannot follow the change of the target, the weights wind up and the loss rises across outer iterations; fixing the weights, or giving the steps more freedom, removes the windup.
\end{conjecture}
The evidence for the conjecture is of two kinds. A run on the square lattice of the benchmark on the device with flicker, recorded in the design record of the method, ran away on one seed in two inside the method's own model: the loss rose from 0.044 to 0.53 between the second and the fourth outer iteration, the weights spread to a ratio of about 1000 and the efficiency fell from 0.50 to 0.03;
that run is the reason of the safeguard and is not re-run here. The ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures the second kind: with the weights fixed to one, or with six steps per outer iteration, the spread on the crosstalk device is lower than with the default. The conjecture is not proved, and the conditions under which the weights help are not characterized.

\subsection{Transient intensity of a shifted pattern}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:blend}
Consider a single trap read in the blend model of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, with a flat input amplitude over $n$ pixels along one axis, and two consecutive holograms $\phi^{(s)}$ and $\phi^{(s+1)}=\phi^{(s)}+2\pi\delta\,j/n$ that display the same pattern shifted by $\delta$ Fourier units, with $\phi_j^{(s)}-\Delta_j^{(s)}$ constant over the pixels (a hologram of one trap in phase at all pixels). At the half-way instant $\alpha=\tfrac12$, read at the position $\delta/2$ from the trap of frame $s$, the
intensity as a fraction of the frame's own intensity is
\begin{equation}
\frac{I(s,\tfrac12)}{I(s)}=\operatorname{sinc}^2\!\Big(\frac\delta2\Big),\qquad \operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x},\label{eq:sinc}
\end{equation}
in the limit of many pixels, and it equals the value $\rho_{0.9}=0.9$ at the shift $\delta_{0.9}=\deltaNine{}$ Fourier units.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $j$ run over $n$ pixels centred on zero. The blend field is $\tfrac12e^{i\phi_j^{(s)}}(1+e^{2\pi i\delta j/n})$. The read-out at the shifted position adds the delay $\Delta_j^{(s)}+\pi\delta j/n$ to the trap's own delay, so the amplitude is $\frac1n\sum_j\tfrac12e^{i(\phi_j^{(s)}-\Delta_j^{(s)})}\big(e^{-i\pi\delta j/n}+e^{+i\pi\delta j/n}\big)=\frac1n\sum_je^{i(\phi_j^{(s)}-\Delta_j^{(s)})}\cos(\pi\delta j/n)$.
With the phase constant the sum is $\frac1n\sum_j\cos(\pi\delta j/n)\to\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\cos(\pi\delta u)\,du=\operatorname{sinc}(\delta/2)$, and the intensity is its square. The value $\delta_{0.9}$ is the root of $\operatorname{sinc}^2(\delta/2)=0.9$, which the measurement script solves by bisection.
\end{proof}

The closed form is a reference for a single trap. In a multi-trap hologram the other traps change between frames as well, and the lowest moving trap can lie below the single-trap value. Two comparisons show how the measurements stand against it: at the peak step of the acceptance task, \accPeakStep{} Fourier units, Eq.~\ref{eq:sinc} gives \sincAcc{}, and the measured lowest transient ratio is \bTwoMean{} on average; at the step bound
of the claim, \claimStepFu{} Fourier units, it gives \sincClaim{}, and the lattice moves of Table~\ref{tab:long}, at peak steps of \longAPeak{} and \longBPeak{} Fourier units, measure medians of \longAMed{} and \longBMed{}. The bound of the claim is therefore not the shift at which the single-trap form reaches $0.9$, which is \deltaNine{} Fourier units, but a more conservative step that was
calibrated on the rearrangement case where the lowest moving trap first reached $0.9$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:frames}
For the minimal-jerk profile the largest shift between consecutive frames of a trap that travels $L$ Fourier units in $S$ intervals is $\delta_{\max}\approx\frac{15}{8}L/S$, and a step bound $\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}$ requires $S\ge\frac{15}{8}L/\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}$ intervals; the number of frames of a sequence, and so its cost, grows linearly with the length of the move.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The profile of Eq.~\ref{eq:minjerk} has $h'(\tau)=30\tau^2(1-\tau)^2$, which is maximal at $\tau=\tfrac12$ with value $\tfrac{30}{16}=\tfrac{15}{8}$. For large $S$ the shift between frames $s$ and $s+1$ is $L(h(\tfrac{s+1}S)-h(\tfrac sS))\approx Lh'(\tfrac sS)/S\le\frac{15}8L/S$. Solving $\frac{15}8L/S\le\delta_{\mathrm{claim}}$ for $S$ gives the bound.
\end{proof}

For the moves of the paper, Eq.~\ref{eq:framecount} gives \accFramesForMove{} frames for the acceptance move, \longAFrames{} and \longBFrames{} frames for the two lattice moves and \cTenFrames{} frames for the longest move of the rearrangement case (Fig.~\ref{fig:b2theory}b), which are the frame counts used in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Phase locking and the phase path}

The lock turns the free optical phases into a prescribed path. Eq.~\ref{eq:targetstep} shows that with a bounded phase step the target of the optimization changes slowly between frames, which a free phase does not guarantee: the fixed point of the weighted iteration determines the trap phases only through the interference of the pixels, and nothing prevents them from jumping between consecutive
frames when the traps move. The ablation measures the consequence (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). The lock has a price: the path of the phases is prescribed by the end phases, and a path along which no hologram can make the traps equal cannot be repaired by the per-frame optimization.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:phasepath}
When the flicker-bounded sequence misses the bound on a long move with the frames of Eq.~\ref{eq:framecount}, the cause is the phase path that the end phases prescribe, not the number of frames or the number of iterations per frame.
\end{conjecture}
The evidence is that the seeds that miss the bound on the lattice moves stay below it when the frames are doubled (the lists of Section~\ref{sec:results}), and that on the rearrangement case a seed collapses to \cTenLowest{} of the mean with the frames of the move while the others reach \cTenMetMin{} to \cTenMetMax{}. The conjecture would be refuted by a seed that misses the bound with the frames of the move and
reaches it with more frames or iterations; the data of this paper contain none. The conditions under which the end phases from phase-fixed WGS give a good path are not characterized.

\subsection{Methods compared}

The published methods are run by the product on the same data, not quoted. For device-aware WGS the baselines are plain GS, weighted GS and phase-fixed WGS, the last at 30 and at 100 iterations, scored on the device; the reference of the comparison is the better of the two phase-fixed runs on the spread and on the efficiency (Eq.~\ref{eq:factor}), because more iterations cannot remove a crosstalk that the model does not contain (Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}).
The ladder methods of the studio are the implementations of the cited algorithms and are checked against the independent library slmsuite~\cite{slmsuite} in the engine's tests. For the flicker-bounded sequence the baseline is linear phase interpolation as described in its source~\cite{lpi}: two independent phase-fixed WGS holograms of $K_0$ iterations, the position, amplitude and optical phase of each trap read from their fields, interpolated linearly over the same frames (the phase by its wrapped difference), and the
transform inverted at every frame by the superposition of the interpolated spots without optimization. It is our implementation of a published description, not the authors' code. The controls are device-aware WGS with the device removed from the loop and the ablated variants of Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}

The metrics are the efficiency and the spread of Eq.~\ref{eq:metrics}, the factor and the efficiency ratio of Eq.~\ref{eq:factor}, the transient ratio of Eq.~\ref{eq:transient} and the per-frame spread, the largest of the relative standard deviations of the trap intensities over the frames of a sequence. The factor of a seed is a ratio, so its mean and interval are
taken on the logarithmic scale: for $n$ seeds with factors $F_i$,
\begin{equation}
\bar F=\exp\Big(\frac1n\sum_i\log F_i\Big),\qquad \bar F\,e^{\pm t_{n-1}s/\sqrt n},\quad s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i\big(\log F_i-\overline{\log F}\big)^2,\label{eq:geomean}
\end{equation}
with $t_{n-1}$ the two-sided 95 percent quantile of Student's $t$ distribution. The transient ratio of a method is summarized by the mean, the sample standard deviation and the interval of the mean, $\bar\rho\pm t_{n-1}s/\sqrt n$, and the comparison of the flicker-bounded sequence with the baseline is a paired difference,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i=\rho_i^{\mathrm{FB}}-\rho_i^{\mathrm{LPI}},\qquad \bar\Delta\pm t_{n-1}\,\frac{s_\Delta}{\sqrt n},\label{eq:paired}
\end{equation}
over the seeds, where the pairing is by seed index only: the flicker-bounded sequence draws its random start from the seed, and the endpoints of the baseline are drawn from an offset seed, so the two methods do not share a random start and the paired interval is not a matched-start comparison. With five seeds the intervals are wide and the tests that they imply are of low power; no $p$-value is reported, and a claim rests on the interval and on the count of seeds that meet a threshold. The medians and ranges are
reported as well, because the distributions are not normal (the factor of device-aware WGS is bounded below by zero and skewed). The benchmark cells report the median and the interquartile range of the studio's protocol.

\subsection{Protocol and ablations}

All methods are deterministic given a seed through the portable generator of the engine, so a rerun reproduces the numbers up to the floating point of the machine's FFT. The seeds are $1,\dots,n$ with $n$ stated per experiment: \nSeedsOne{} for the crosstalk device, \nSeedsOneFull{} for the full device, \nSeedsTwo{} for the acceptance task, \longSeeds{} for the long lattice moves, \cTenSeeds{} for the rearrangement case, and \ablBN{}, \ablBFN{} and \ablTN{}
for the ablation of device-aware WGS on the crosstalk device, of device-aware WGS on the full device and of the flicker-bounded sequence. The budget of device-aware WGS is \parOuter{} outer iterations after \parWarm{} warm iterations; that of the flicker-bounded sequence is \parFrames{} frames of \parFrameIters{} iterations after a cold start of \parColdIters{}. The ablation is planned for the components that the design records name as the rationale of each method (H6 to H8): the weight update, the device-aware phase step, the safeguard and the number of steps for device-aware WGS;
the trajectory lock, the per-frame optimization, the number of iterations per frame and the time profile for the flicker-bounded sequence. Each variant removes one component and keeps the others, on the same seeds and tasks as the main result, and the variant with all components on is checked against the engine's module.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the methods behave as they do}

The ideal-model solver equalizes a field that the device does not produce (Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}), and the device-aware step equalizes the field that is scored. The ablation separates two contributions: the amplitudes read on the device give a factor of about two on their own, and the gradient steps through the crosstalk adjoint give the rest. The control with the device removed from the loop confirms that the steps are not the cause: the same steps on the ideal model leave the spread at the baseline.
For the flicker-bounded sequence the transient ratio is limited by the shift between consecutive frames, as the closed form of Eq.~\ref{eq:sinc} states, and the optimization and the lock make the frames individually uniform and mutually continuous, so that a bound on the step is a bound on the dip. The no-optimization variant fails with the same closed-form shifts as the full method, so the gain of the per-frame optimization is the uniformity of the frames, not a smaller shift.

\subsection{Failure cases and negative results}

Four results are negative and they bound what the paper claims. First, device-aware WGS fails the factor on the square lattice and on the large array of the benchmark's device (it keeps its start on the square lattice and lowers the spread by \benchOneDevLastFactor{} on the large array, short of two), so the success on the lattice behind a crosstalk device is specific to that device (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Second, the weights of device-aware WGS hurt on the acceptance task and on the full device, where they were supposed to help, and are mixed on the benchmark lattices (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). Third, on the rearrangement case at an equal number of frames linear phase interpolation leads the flicker-bounded sequence on every seed, and the flicker-bounded sequence needs the
end phases and the frames of Eq.~\ref{eq:framecount} to be competitive there. Fourth, the flicker-bounded sequence collapses on a seed of the rearrangement case and on seeds of the lattice moves, and more frames do not recover them. The last result is the cleanest statement of the limit of the lock: where the end phases prescribe a bad path, the per-frame optimization cannot repair it. A method that chose the end phases jointly with the frames would remove the limit; we did not build one.

\subsection{Relation to the published methods}

Two published results frame what the paper adds. Schroff et al.\ put the crosstalk model inside conjugate-gradient minimization, lowered the error of a shaped potential, paid about 20 percent of the efficiency and ran about six times longer~\cite{schroff}. The variant of device-aware WGS without the weight update is the point-trap counterpart of that idea in our engine, and on the acceptance task it reaches a median spread of \ablBNoWeightsSig{} percent at an efficiency ratio of \ablBNoWeightsEff{} to the baseline, against \ablBFullSig{} percent for the method with its weights. The two tasks, devices and metrics differ, so the numbers are not comparable, and the time per iteration was not measured here; what the comparison supports is only that the device model inside a conjugate-gradient step, and not the weighted-GS structure around it, carries the gain on this task. The phase-fixed rule of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2019} remains the right start and the right baseline: it is the reference of every factor in this paper, and the warm start of device-aware WGS uses that rule.

For sequences the comparison is with the linear phase interpolation of Knottnerus et al.~\cite{lpi}. The per-frame optimization with locked phases lifts the lowest moving trap above the bound on the acceptance task under our blend model, but the source measured on hardware and our model is our own definition, so the paper establishes an ordering of the two methods under one model and not an improvement on the hardware of the source. Where the end phases prescribe a bad path, as on seeds of the rearrangement case, the proposed sequence collapses, and at an equal number of frames on that case linear phase interpolation leads; the proposed sequence is not to be used there without the frames and the end phases of Eq.~\ref{eq:framecount}.

\subsection{What the results imply for use}

The results suggest a division of labour. For a static array behind a device whose crosstalk is modelled, the device-aware phase step is the part to adopt, and the evidence of this paper favours it without the weights on the acceptance task and on the full device and is mixed on the benchmark lattices; the safeguard is cheap insurance on geometries where the last iterate loses light. For a rearrangement with moves of a few Fourier units the flicker-bounded sequence keeps the lowest trap near its mean with a number of frames that follows from Eq.~\ref{eq:framecount}; for long moves
the number of frames, and with it the cost, grows linearly, and the choice of end phases decides the outcome seed by seed. Neither method replaces camera feedback: the paper measures hologram quality in a model, and a flat hologram on the device model is a better start for feedback, not a substitute for it. What would change these conclusions is a measured device model: with a different kernel, a different quantizer or a measured flicker the factors would change, and the sensitivity of device-aware WGS to a mismatched kernel was not tested.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\textbf{Internal validity.} The seeds are few: \nSeedsOne{} for the crosstalk device, \nSeedsOneFull{} for the full device and for the long-move statistics up to \longSeeds{}, so the intervals are wide and no significance test is reported. The device-aware WGS cells of the benchmark hold two seeds, so the geometry results are ranges. The step bound of the flicker-bounded sequence was calibrated on the rearrangement case and is tested out of sample only on the lattice moves. The baseline of linear phase interpolation is our implementation of a published
description, scored by our own transient model, not the authors' code on their hardware; the reference value of the source (above $0.70$ of the intensity on hardware) is higher than the value that the baseline scores here, so the absolute values of the two are not comparable. The variants of the ablation are re-implementations of the loops of the modules and were verified to reproduce them, so the ablated rows are not a different method.

\textbf{External validity.} Every result is simulated. The crosstalk parameters come from a published fit on another device, and their frequency unit is carried over from the model and is not verified against the source; the \parBits{}-bit quantizer, the fill factor and the flicker have no published fit in this model, and random flicker is scored and not modelled by the optimizer. An instrument with another kernel would give another factor. The tasks are lattices of equal traps on bins at one wavelength and one pitch, a Gaussian beam and no aberrations; the benchmark adds other geometries on one device, and the three-dimensional and shaped-field targets are outside the scope of device-aware WGS.

\textbf{Construct validity.} The spread and the efficiency are model quantities, and the factor measures them against phase-fixed WGS without camera feedback, whereas the experiments close the gap with feedback; a method that is two times flatter before feedback may or may not need fewer feedback steps, and that was not measured. The transient ratio is defined by the blend model of the SLM field between frames, which omits the temporal response and the refresh of the device; it establishes an ordering of the two methods under one model
and is not a prediction of atom survival. A bound of $0.9$ on the transient ratio is a design threshold and is not derived from the survival of atoms.

\subsection{Scope and what is not claimed}

Neither method is shown to improve a camera-corrected experiment or the survival of atoms. Device-aware WGS is built for traps on FFT bins at the focal plane without vortices and costs about $u^2$ times a plain iteration per step, so it is baked and not live. The authors' conjugate-gradient minimization with a crosstalk model, with its automatic differentiation and its shaped potentials, is not run; the variant of device-aware WGS without the weight update is the nearest thing to it that the paper runs, on point traps, and the source reports about ten minutes per hologram for its own method. The flicker-bounded sequence is shown on lattices and one rearrangement case in two dimensions, with end phases from phase-fixed WGS,
and it does not choose the assignment of atoms to sites. The time per iteration of device-aware WGS against the baseline was not measured, and the claim that device-aware WGS is within a factor of three of the baseline in time is not made. The paper also does not test the sensitivity of device-aware WGS to a kernel that differs from the one the device displays, which is the case in any real instrument whose kernel is fitted with error; a hologram optimized on a wrong kernel would carry part of the crosstalk error that the method removes in the model.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

The code, the data and the committed artifacts are in the repository \url{https://github.com/fsantibanezleal/CEFOP_DinHot} at release \engineRelease{} (Apache-2.0); the studio runs at \url{https://dinhot.fasl-work.com}. Every number, table and figure of the paper is produced by two scripts from the repository root. The measurement script \path{manuscripts/device-aware-flicker-bounded/data/measure.py} runs the methods of the engine and writes
\texttt{beyond.json}, the macro file read by the text and the result tables; with no argument it measures every part, with \texttt{--parts=ablation} it measures only the ablation and keeps the stored parts (the main measurements of the two methods took about \mainMinutes{} minutes in total and this ablation run about \ablSeconds{} minutes on the development machine, CPU only, with five seeds for device-aware WGS on the crosstalk device, three on the full device and five for the flicker-bounded sequence), and with \texttt{--macros-only} it rewrites the macros and the tables from the stored measurements. The figure script \path{manuscripts/device-aware-flicker-bounded/figures/make_figs.py} writes every figure as a
vector PDF from \texttt{beyond.json}. The document builds with three passes of \texttt{pdflatex} from \texttt{tex/}. The benchmark file and the committed manifests that the script reads are in \texttt{data/derived/}. The acceptance tests of the two methods are \texttt{test\_b1\_device\_wgs.py} and \texttt{test\_b2\_flicker\_bounded.py}, and the ablated variants are checked against the modules inside the measurement script. The comparison of the weight update on the benchmark lattices (Table~\ref{tab:ablb1geo}) is written by \path{manuscripts/device-aware-flicker-bounded/data/b1_weights_geo.py}, one process per geometry (\texttt{run G1} to \texttt{run G4}, about half an hour each) and then \texttt{write}. All methods are deterministic given a seed, so a rerun reproduces the numbers up to the floating point of the machine's FFT.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Putting the pixel-crosstalk kernel and the phase quantizer inside the phase update of weighted GS lowers the trap-intensity spread on the device by a geometric-mean factor of \ratioOneGm{} (95 percent interval \ratioOneCiLo{} to \ratioOneCiHi{}) on a $10\times10$ lattice behind a crosstalk device, at \effRatioOne{} of the baseline efficiency, and the gain disappears when the device model is removed from the loop. The gain does not carry to every geometry: on the benchmark's device with flicker the method meets both thresholds on the ring and the random set, keeps its start (factors near one) on the square lattice and the quasi-periodic set, and lowers the spread by \benchOneDevLastFactor{} on the large set, short of the threshold of two. The ablation shows that the
gradient step through the device, and not the weights, produces the gain; the variant without the weights has the lower spread on the acceptance task and the full device and a mixed one on the benchmark lattices, so the weights are the first component to reconsider. Optimizing each frame with locked, smoothly varying trap phases keeps the moving traps of the acceptance task at a mean of \bTwoMean{} of their intensity (interval \bTwoCiLo{} to \bTwoCiHi{}) where linear phase interpolation falls to \lpiMean{}; with the frames that keep the peak step at most \claimStepFu{} Fourier units it meets the bound on most seeds of longer moves and not on all, and at an equal number of frames on a long rearrangement linear phase interpolation leads.
The next steps are the measurement on a device with a fitted model and a camera in the loop, the ablation of device-aware WGS on the benchmark lattices with a phase-fixed WGS reference at the same budget, to test whether dropping the weights removes the failure of H3 (Table~\ref{tab:ablb1geo} has no such reference), and a choice of end phases that is joint with the frames.
